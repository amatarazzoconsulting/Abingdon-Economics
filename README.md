# Abingdon-Economics
# The Appalachian Gateway: A Regional Economic Expansion Strategy for Southwest Virginia
## By Anthony Matarazzo (c) 2026


## Book Overview

**Subtitle**: Industrial Diversification, Investment Opportunities, and Partnership Guide for Abingdon, Bristol, and Surrounding Communities

**Target Audience**: Regional economic development decision-makers, industry investors, manufacturing executives, local government planners

**Core Thesis**: The Abingdon-Bristol corridor stands at a critical window of transition from a resource-based economy to a diversified, high-value-added economy. Success depends on advancing three fronts simultaneously: reinforcing the technical moat of medium-voltage switchgear manufacturing, activating the resource depth of agriculture and forestry, and building a partnership network that connects innovation resources to industrial needs.


# Chapter One: Abingdon and Bristol — Economic Foundations and Transition Pressures

Abingdon and Bristol sit nestled within the Appalachian Mountains of Southwest Virginia, and the economic fate of this region has experienced dramatic swings over the past century. Abingdon's population stood at approximately 8,431 in 2024, with an employment structure in which retail trade accounts for 15.9 percent, educational services 13.1 percent, and health care and social assistance 12.5 percent. Bristol's employment landscape differs somewhat, with manufacturing leading at 15.5 percent, followed by retail trade at 14.4 percent and health care and social assistance at 12.7 percent. Together, the data from these two cities paint a portrait of a post-industrial small-city cluster: service industries provide the bulk of employment but at limited wage levels, while manufacturing remains the primary source of high-wage jobs but faces transition pressures.

From a longer historical perspective, this region has endured the violent shocks of structural decline. Between 1990 and 2016, agricultural employment in Southwest Virginia fell by 26 percent, mining by 69 percent, and manufacturing by 46 percent. These three former economic pillars contracted in succession, leaving an employment gap that has yet to be filled. The closure of coal mines eliminated not only direct mining jobs but also the transportation, equipment maintenance, and supply chain employment that supported them. The offshoring of textile and furniture manufacturing transferred thousands of factory jobs overseas. This decline process shaped the core characteristics of the local labor market: skills mismatch, population outmigration, and an intense desire for new industrial opportunities.

Washington County's manufacturing base demonstrates remarkable diversity, which provides a foundation for economic resilience. The county's largest manufacturers include Universal Fibers, with 530 employees producing synthetic fibers; Utility Trailer of Glade Spring, with 575 employees manufacturing dry freight vans; Steel Fab, with 255 employees producing pressure vessels; and Paramount Manufacturing, with 185 employees making molded truck components. The presence of these companies demonstrates that the region possesses the infrastructure, labor pool, and supply chain networks to support mid-sized manufacturing operations. They also constitute "anchor points" for attracting new manufacturers—new entrants can share supplier bases, logistics corridors, and skilled worker pools.

Agriculture occupies an underappreciated but critically important position in Washington County's economy. According to county data, agriculture and related industries rank as the number one industry in Washington County by gross revenue, with product sales market value reaching $76.5 million. Beef cattle production alone contributes approximately $120 million annually to the county's economy, and the region's natural advantages in grass-based cattle production are significant. Gene Copenhaver, a Washington County beef farmer and president-elect of the National Cattlemen's Beef Association, notes that "we have some of the best grazing in the world here, so our cost of gain is a lot cheaper." This competitive advantage means the region can produce beef at lower cost than many competing areas, creating a foundation for a robust local beef processing industry.

The timber and logging industry represents another significant economic resource that remains underexploited. Appalachian Sustainable Development, a nonprofit based in Abingdon, has demonstrated that processing timber locally rather than shipping raw logs out of the region creates significantly more jobs and economic value. The organization's Sustainable Woods Processing Center in Castlewood, Virginia, has the capacity to process 200,000 board feet of lumber annually, turning logs into boards, flooring, cabinets, and other finished products. The center employs sustainable harvesting practices that leave the best trees standing, creating a renewable resource that can be harvested selectively every 12 years or so while maintaining forest health. The contrast with the old model is instructive: the Louisiana Pacific waferboard factory in Dungannon, Virginia, which closed abruptly in 1996, relied on extensive clear-cutting for cheap timber and established no roots in the community; when it closed, nearly 100 workers lost their jobs along with the loggers who supplied the plant.

The workforce development challenge is significant but manageable. The region has a population with deep roots in agriculture and forestry, and many workers possess practical skills that are transferable to food processing and wood products manufacturing. The wages in these sectors are competitive with other local employment: food batchmakers in the Mount Rogers region earn a mean hourly wage of $25.87, higher than the Virginia average of $21.11, while packaging and filling machine operators earn $22.40 per hour. These wages, combined with the lower cost of living in the region, provide a reasonable standard of living for workers and their families. The challenge is to connect workers with the training and credentials needed to access these jobs, and to attract new workers to the region as the economy expands.

The economic development infrastructure in the region is well-established and can serve as a platform for expansion. The Virginia Economic Development Partnership provides statewide resources for business recruitment and expansion. The Mount Rogers Planning District Commission serves as the regional planning organization, coordinating economic development efforts across multiple jurisdictions. The Washington County Economic Development office and the Bristol Virginia Economic Development office provide local support for business retention, expansion, and recruitment. These organizations can help investors and companies navigate the incentives, site selection, workforce training, and permitting processes required to establish or expand operations in the region.

The competitive landscape for economic development in the region includes several other communities in Southwest Virginia and Northeast Tennessee that are pursuing similar strategies. Kingsport, Tennessee, has a strong advanced manufacturing base anchored by Eastman Chemical. Johnson City, Tennessee, has invested heavily in healthcare and higher education, with East Tennessee State University serving as a major employer and research institution. Wytheville, Virginia, has developed a successful industrial park and attracted manufacturers in the automotive and aerospace sectors. These communities are competitors for the same pool of manufacturing investment, but they are also potential partners in regional initiatives that can benefit the entire corridor.

The infrastructure assets of the region include Interstate 81, which runs through the heart of the Abingdon-Bristol corridor and provides direct access to markets in the Northeast, Southeast, and Midwest. The region is served by Norfolk Southern Railway, which provides freight rail service to industrial sites. The Tri-Cities Regional Airport provides commercial air service with connections to major hubs. The region's electric power infrastructure is robust, with multiple transmission lines and substations providing reliable service to industrial customers. These infrastructure assets are a competitive advantage for the region and can support a wide range of manufacturing and processing operations.

The demographic trends in the region present both challenges and opportunities. Like many rural regions in Appalachia, Southwest Virginia has experienced population decline and aging as young people leave for opportunities elsewhere. This creates a tight labor market for existing employers and makes it difficult to attract new employers who need a large workforce. However, the region also has a strong work ethic, low turnover rates, and a quality of life that appeals to workers who value outdoor recreation, community connections, and a slower pace of life. The success of economic development efforts will depend on attracting workers who value these attributes and on creating opportunities that allow young people to build careers in the region.

The education and training infrastructure in the region includes Virginia Highlands Community College, which provides workforce training and associate degree programs in technical fields. The Southwest Virginia Higher Education Center in Abingdon offers bachelor's and master's degree programs from multiple Virginia universities. The region's high schools provide career and technical education programs that prepare students for entry into the workforce or further education. These institutions are critical partners in workforce development and can be leveraged to provide customized training for new and expanding employers.

The healthcare sector is a major employer in the region and a source of economic stability. Ballad Health, the region's dominant healthcare provider, operates Johnston Memorial Hospital in Abingdon and Bristol Regional Medical Center in Bristol, employing thousands of workers across the region. The healthcare sector has been one of the most reliable job creators in the region, adding jobs even during economic downturns. The presence of a strong healthcare sector is also an asset for economic development, as companies considering relocation evaluate the quality and accessibility of healthcare services for their employees.

The retail sector in the region faces structural challenges from e-commerce competition and changing consumer preferences. The decline of traditional shopping malls and the growth of online retail have reduced employment in brick-and-mortar retail, though some segments such as grocery, home improvement, and specialty retail have remained stable. The region's downtown areas in Abingdon and Bristol have invested in revitalization efforts, creating vibrant commercial districts that attract tourists and local residents. These efforts can be expanded to support small business development and entrepreneurship, creating jobs that are less vulnerable to offshoring and automation.

The tourism and hospitality sector benefits from the region's natural and cultural attractions. The Virginia Creeper Trail, a 35-mile rail-trail that runs from Abingdon to Whitetop, attracts cyclists and outdoor enthusiasts from across the region and beyond. The Barter Theatre in Abingdon, the state theatre of Virginia, draws audiences for professional productions throughout the year. Bristol's reputation as the "Birthplace of Country Music" supports a music tourism industry anchored by the Birthplace of Country Music Museum. These attractions create jobs in hospitality, food service, and retail, and they enhance the region's quality of life, making it more attractive to workers and entrepreneurs.

The transition from a resource-based economy to a diversified economy requires strategic investments in infrastructure, workforce development, and business attraction. The region's leaders have recognized this imperative and have developed plans and initiatives to guide the transition. The question is not whether the region can diversify but whether it can do so quickly enough to offset the losses from declining sectors and create opportunities for the next generation. The chapters that follow examine specific opportunities in manufacturing, agriculture, forestry, and technology, and identify the partners and investors who can help the region realize its potential.


# Chapter Two: Advanced Manufacturing — Switchgear, Automation, and Competitive Strategy

The advanced manufacturing sector in the Abingdon-Bristol region is anchored by Electro-Mechanical, LLC, a manufacturer of medium-voltage switchgear headquartered in Bristol, Virginia. The company's Federal Pacific brand is well-known in the industry, and its products are used in data centers, hospitals, universities, and industrial facilities across the United States. The company's $16.5 million expansion, which includes a new 200,000-square-foot facility and the addition of over 100 new jobs, positions it to compete more effectively with larger competitors such as Siemens, Schneider Electric, and ABB. The expansion is also a vote of confidence in the region's workforce and business climate, and it demonstrates that mid-sized manufacturers can thrive in Southwest Virginia.

The medium-voltage switchgear industry is in the midst of a technological transition driven by environmental regulations and digitalization. The phase-out of sulfur hexafluoride (SF6), a potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential approximately 24,300 times that of carbon dioxide, is creating opportunities for manufacturers that have developed SF6-free alternatives. Electro-Mechanical's competitors, including Siemens, Schneider Electric, and ABB, have all announced commitments to eliminate SF6 from their medium-voltage switchgear portfolios by 2025. Electro-Mechanical must match these commitments or risk losing customers who are subject to increasingly stringent environmental regulations. The development of SF6-free switchgear requires investment in research and development, testing, and certification, but it also creates an opportunity to differentiate the company's products in a crowded market.

The digitalization of switchgear is creating a second axis of competition. Modern switchgear incorporates sensors that monitor temperature, current, voltage, partial discharge, and other parameters, and communicates this data to cloud-based analytics platforms that can predict failures and optimize operation. The integration of digital capabilities requires expertise in software development, data analytics, and cybersecurity that many traditional switchgear manufacturers lack. Electro-Mechanical can build these capabilities internally, partner with software companies, or acquire technology companies that have the needed expertise. The choice will determine the company's competitive position in the coming decade.

The workforce requirements for advanced manufacturing have shifted dramatically in recent years. Modern manufacturing facilities require workers with skills in programming, robotics, data analysis, and quality management, not just manual dexterity. The region's community colleges and workforce development programs must adapt to meet these needs, providing training in advanced manufacturing technologies that prepare workers for the jobs of the future. The partnership between manufacturers, educational institutions, and economic development organizations is essential to developing the talent pipeline that will support the growth of the advanced manufacturing sector.

The automation of manufacturing is a strategic imperative for manufacturers in high-cost labor markets. The United States has higher labor costs than many competing countries, which puts domestic manufacturers at a disadvantage in labor-intensive industries. Automation can reduce labor costs, improve quality, and increase throughput, making domestic manufacturing more competitive. The automation of switchgear manufacturing includes computer numerical control machining, robotic welding, automated assembly, and computerized testing. These technologies reduce the number of workers required for some tasks but increase the demand for workers with the skills to program, maintain, and troubleshoot automated systems.

The supply chain for switchgear manufacturing is global, with components sourced from many countries. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the vulnerability of global supply chains, and many manufacturers are now seeking to diversify their sources and increase domestic production of critical components. Electro-Mechanical's expansion in Bristol is partly a response to this trend, as it seeks to increase its control over its supply chain and reduce its dependence on foreign suppliers. The region's location on Interstate 81, with access to major markets and transportation networks, makes it an attractive location for suppliers who want to serve the switchgear industry and other manufacturers in the region.

The competition with Siemens is particularly relevant because Siemens is a major player in the medium-voltage switchgear market and is also investing heavily in SF6-free technology. Siemens Energy has announced that it will eliminate SF6 from its medium-voltage switchgear portfolio by 2025, and it is investing in the development of vacuum interrupters and clean-air insulation. Siemens also has a strong presence in the digitalization of switchgear, with its Xcelerator platform that provides software and services for the operation and maintenance of electrical equipment. Competing with Siemens requires not only technical excellence in the hardware but also the ability to provide software and services that add value for customers.

The strategy for Electro-Mechanical to compete with Siemens and other large competitors should focus on several key areas. First, the company should continue to invest in research and development, particularly in SF6-free technology and digital integration. The company should develop its own intellectual property in these areas and protect it with patents. Second, the company should invest in manufacturing automation to reduce costs and improve quality. The new facility in Bristol is an opportunity to implement state-of-the-art automation, including robotic welding, automated assembly, and computerized testing. Third, the company should develop partnerships with software companies, sensor manufacturers, and other technology providers to accelerate its digital capabilities. Fourth, the company should focus on customer segments where it has a competitive advantage, such as data centers, healthcare facilities, and industrial plants, and where the need for reliable, customizable switchgear is high.

The advanced manufacturing sector in the region extends beyond switchgear to include a diverse range of companies. Universal Fibers in Bristol produces synthetic fibers for carpets and textiles, employing 530 workers. Utility Trailer in Glade Spring manufactures dry freight vans, employing 575 workers. Steel Fab in Bristol produces pressure vessels and heat exchangers, employing 255 workers. Paramount Manufacturing in Bristol makes molded truck components, employing 185 workers. These companies demonstrate the diversity of the region's manufacturing base and its ability to support a range of industrial processes, from fiber extrusion to metal fabrication to plastics molding.

The growth of the advanced manufacturing sector depends on the availability of industrial sites with the infrastructure needed to support modern manufacturing operations. The region has several industrial parks, including the Bristol Virginia Industrial Park, the Washington County Industrial Park, and the Highlands Business Park in Abingdon. These parks offer sites with utilities, roads, and other infrastructure needed for manufacturing. The region should continue to invest in industrial site development to ensure that it has a ready inventory of sites that can accommodate the needs of expanding and relocating companies.

The incentives available for manufacturing investment in Virginia include the Virginia Jobs Investment Program, which provides funding for workforce training and retraining; the Virginia Investment Performance Grant, which provides cash grants based on capital investment and job creation; and the Commonwealth's Development Opportunity Fund, which provides grants to localities for economic development projects. These programs can reduce the cost of establishing or expanding manufacturing operations in the region and improve the return on investment for companies considering relocation.

The competitive landscape for manufacturing investment includes communities across the Southeast and Midwest that offer lower costs, better infrastructure, and more generous incentives. The region must differentiate itself by emphasizing its strengths: a skilled workforce with a strong work ethic, a strategic location on Interstate 81, a low cost of living, and a quality of life that appeals to workers and executives alike. The region's success in attracting and retaining manufacturing investment will depend on its ability to tell its story effectively and to deliver on the promises it makes to companies.

The future of advanced manufacturing in the region is likely to involve greater integration of digital technologies, more automation, and a focus on high-value products that cannot be easily replicated by competitors. The region should position itself as a center for advanced manufacturing in Southwest Virginia, building on the presence of Electro-Mechanical and other manufacturers to attract suppliers, service providers, and complementary businesses. The development of a manufacturing cluster can create a self-reinforcing cycle of investment and growth that benefits the entire region.

For investors, the advanced manufacturing sector in the region offers several attractive characteristics. The industry is capital-intensive, creating barriers to entry that limit competition. The products are essential to the functioning of modern society, creating stable demand. The region's cost structure is competitive with other locations in the United States and globally. And the presence of established manufacturers such as Electro-Mechanical provides a foundation for growth and diversification. The key is to identify specific opportunities where the region's resources and capabilities align with market demand, and to execute those opportunities with disciplined management and realistic financial projections.


# Chapter Three: Agriculture and Food Processing — From Raw Commodities to Value-Added Products

The agricultural sector in Washington County and the surrounding region is experiencing a historic price boom that is injecting hundreds of millions of dollars into the local economy. Beef cattle production alone contributes approximately $120 million annually to Washington County's economy, and sustained increases in beef prices have created what agricultural extension agents describe as "prices that no one has ever seen before." The wholesale price for fed cattle surged to a record $225 per hundredweight during the summer of 2025, compared to the previous high of around $180 per hundredweight. This price boom is driven by a combination of higher domestic demand, a decline in the national cattle herd due to Western droughts, and supply disruptions from a screw worm infestation in Mexican cattle herds that has not yet reached the United States.

The region's natural advantages in cattle production are significant and underappreciated. Gene Copenhaver, a Washington County beef farmer and president-elect of the National Cattlemen's Beef Association, notes that "we have some of the best grazing in the world here, so our cost of gain is a lot cheaper." This competitive advantage in grass-based cattle production means that the region can produce beef at lower cost than many competing areas, creating a foundation for a robust local beef processing industry. Currently, much of the cattle raised in the region is shipped to out-of-state feedlots and processing facilities, meaning the value-added processing—and the jobs that come with it—occurs elsewhere.

The meat processing and packaging industry represents the most direct path to adding value to the region's cattle production. A meat processing facility that slaughters, cuts, and packages beef for local and regional markets would create jobs in processing, packaging, cold storage, and distribution. The Frichtl Processing and Slaughtering facility in Dieterich, Illinois, provides a model for how this can work: a $2.5 million investment created a 9,227-square-foot facility that processes beef, hogs, and deer, with a retail store that provides locally sourced meat products to the community. This facility received state incentives through the EDGE for Startups program and created six new full-time jobs, while also addressing food access issues in an area identified as a food desert.

The packaged food manufacturing sector represents a second tier of opportunity that can be built on the region's agricultural base. Shearer's Foods, a major snack manufacturer with a facility in Bristol, Virginia, demonstrates that food processing can be a significant employer in the region. The company operates 16 locations across North America and employs more than 5,000 team members, producing salty snacks, cookies, and crackers for private label and co-manufacturing clients. Food processing operators at the Bristol facility earn approximately $18.14 per hour, with sanitation technicians earning $17.60 per hour. These wages provide stable employment for workers who may not have the specialized skills required for advanced manufacturing roles.

The connection between local agriculture and food processing is the key to maximizing economic impact. A packaged food manufacturer that sources ingredients locally—whether beef, grains, or produce—creates a multiplier effect that benefits farmers, processors, and the broader community. The Michigan Department of Agriculture's experience with Peterson Brands illustrates this principle: the company relocated juice production from Florida to Michigan, creating 82 jobs and increasing apple usage by approximately 40 million pounds annually from local growers. By co-locating processing with agricultural production, the company reduced transportation costs, improved supply chain reliability, and created a market for fruit that would otherwise go unpicked.

The potential for craft beverage production using local agricultural products is an emerging opportunity. The barley malting study for Northeast Tennessee and Southwest Virginia examined the feasibility of growing, processing, and malting grains in the region for beer production. While the study was completed in 2016, the craft beverage industry has continued to grow, and the region's agricultural resources—including barley, wheat, and specialty crops—could support a local malting and brewing ecosystem. The success of Stone Ledge Cider in Augusta County demonstrates that craft beverage facilities can create jobs and expand markets for local agricultural producers. Stone Ledge Cider received $10,000 in Agriculture and Forestry Industries Development Fund (AFID) funds and committed to purchasing $166,858 in Virginia-grown apples and honey.

The state incentives available for agriculture and forestry value-added processing can reduce the capital requirements and improve returns for investors. Virginia's Agriculture and Forestry Industries Development Fund (AFID) provides grants to localities for businesses that add value to Virginia-grown agriculture and forestry products. The AFID Facilities Grant program has supported projects such as Stone Ledge Cider, while the AFID Infrastructure Grant program has awarded nearly $2 million to 60 projects since 2021, funding community infrastructure that supports local food production and sustainable agriculture. These programs provide a model for how state and local governments can support value-added agricultural processing in the Abingdon-Bristol region.

The workforce development needs of the food processing industry are different from those of advanced manufacturing but equally important. Food processing requires workers with knowledge of food safety, sanitation, quality control, and production processes. The region's community colleges and workforce development programs can provide training in these areas, preparing workers for careers in food processing. The wages in food processing are competitive with other local employment, and the industry offers opportunities for advancement from entry-level production positions to supervisory and management roles.

The infrastructure requirements for food processing include reliable utilities, adequate water supply, wastewater treatment, and cold storage. The region has several sites that could accommodate food processing operations, including sites in industrial parks with access to utilities and transportation. The development of a food processing cluster would require investment in specialized infrastructure, such as cold storage and distribution facilities, that could be shared by multiple companies. The region's economic development organizations can play a role in facilitating this investment and attracting food processing companies to the region.

The competition for food processing investment includes communities across the Southeast that offer lower costs, better infrastructure, and access to larger markets. The region must differentiate itself by emphasizing its strengths: a local agricultural base that can supply raw materials, a strategic location on Interstate 81 with access to markets in the Northeast and Southeast, and a workforce with experience in food production and agriculture. The region's success in attracting food processing investment will depend on its ability to demonstrate these advantages to companies considering relocation.

The beef price boom provides a window of opportunity for strategic investment in processing infrastructure. Farmers who are currently enjoying record prices have the financial capacity to invest in value-added ventures, and the region's agricultural extension services and economic development organizations can help facilitate these investments. The risk is that the price boom will be temporary—cattle markets are notoriously cyclical—and that investments made at the peak of the cycle will struggle when prices decline. This argues for a diversified approach that combines cattle processing with other agricultural and forestry value-added activities, creating a portfolio of revenue streams that can weather market fluctuations.

The future of agriculture in the region is likely to involve greater consolidation of farms, increased use of technology, and a focus on high-value products that can be sold directly to consumers or to specialty markets. The region's small farms may struggle to compete with large-scale operations in the Midwest and West, but they can succeed by focusing on niche markets such as grass-fed beef, organic produce, and craft beverages. The development of local food systems, including farmers markets, community-supported agriculture, and farm-to-table restaurants, can create markets for local products and support the viability of small farms.

For investors, the agricultural and food processing sectors offer several attractive characteristics. The industries are essential—people need to eat—creating stable demand. The region's natural advantages in cattle production and other agricultural products give it a competitive cost position. And the state incentive programs for agriculture and forestry value-added processing reduce the capital requirements and improve returns. The key is to identify specific opportunities where the region's resources align with market demand, and to execute those opportunities with disciplined management and realistic financial projections.


# Chapter Four: Timber, Logging, and Wood Products — The Sustainable Forest Economy

The timber and logging industry in Southwest Virginia represents a significant economic resource that is currently underexploited. The region's forests are among the most productive hardwood forests in the world, and the sustainable management of these forests can provide a renewable source of income for landowners, loggers, and wood products manufacturers. Appalachian Sustainable Development, a nonprofit based in Abingdon, has demonstrated that processing timber locally rather than shipping raw logs out of the region creates significantly more jobs and economic value. The organization's Sustainable Woods Processing Center in Castlewood, Virginia, has the capacity to process 200,000 board feet of lumber annually, turning logs into boards, flooring, cabinets, and other finished products.

The contrast between the old and new models of timber processing is instructive. The Louisiana Pacific waferboard factory in Dungannon, Virginia, which closed abruptly in 1996, relied on extensive clear-cutting for cheap timber and established no roots in the community; when it closed, nearly 100 workers lost their jobs along with the loggers who supplied the plant. The Appalachian Sustainable Development model, by contrast, adds value locally through sawing, drying, and manufacturing, creating multiple layers of employment and ensuring that the economic benefits remain in the community. This model also creates incentives for long-term forest management, as landowners who practice sustainable harvesting can expect repeated harvests rather than a single clear-cut payout.

The wood products industry in the region has a long history and a skilled workforce that can be leveraged for expansion. Hardwood saw-log production in the southern Appalachians has been relatively stable, with prices for hardwood saw logs rising at an average annual rate of 2 to 3 percent over the past two decades, reflecting their scarcity value. The USDA Forest Service projects that U.S. hardwood harvest will increase by 52 percent between 1991 and 2040, with the South consistently contributing 58 percent of the total. This projected growth represents an opportunity for the region to expand its wood processing capacity and capture more of the value chain, from sawmilling to furniture manufacturing to engineered wood products.

The sustainable forestry model practiced by Appalachian Sustainable Development has several advantages over conventional clear-cutting. Selective harvesting leaves the best trees standing, maintaining the health and biodiversity of the forest. The forest regenerates naturally, eliminating the need for replanting. The soil is disturbed less, reducing erosion and protecting water quality. And the forest remains visually attractive, preserving its value for recreation and tourism. These advantages are increasingly recognized by landowners, consumers, and regulators, creating a market for sustainably produced wood products that command premium prices.

The infrastructure for wood products manufacturing in the region includes sawmills, drying kilns, planing mills, and finishing operations. The Sustainable Woods Processing Center in Castlewood is one example of a facility that provides these services. The region has other sawmills and wood products manufacturers that serve local and regional markets. The development of a wood products cluster would require investment in additional capacity, particularly in secondary processing such as furniture manufacturing, cabinet making, and engineered wood products. These operations create more jobs per unit of timber than primary processing and capture more of the value chain.

The competition for wood products manufacturing investment includes communities across the Southeast that offer lower costs, better infrastructure, and access to larger markets. The region must differentiate itself by emphasizing its strengths: a sustainable supply of high-quality hardwood timber, a skilled workforce with experience in wood products manufacturing, and a strategic location on Interstate 81 with access to markets in the Northeast and Southeast. The region's success in attracting wood products investment will depend on its ability to demonstrate these advantages to companies considering relocation.

The state incentives available for forestry and wood products manufacturing include the Agriculture and Forestry Industries Development Fund (AFID), which provides grants to localities for businesses that add value to Virginia-grown forestry products. The AFID program has supported projects such as the Sustainable Woods Processing Center in Castlewood, which received funding to expand its operations. These programs can reduce the capital requirements and improve returns for investors considering wood products manufacturing in the region.

The workforce development needs of the wood products industry include skills in sawmill operation, kiln drying, wood grading, and furniture manufacturing. The region's community colleges and workforce development programs can provide training in these areas, preparing workers for careers in the wood products industry. The wages in wood products manufacturing are competitive with other local employment, and the industry offers opportunities for advancement from entry-level production positions to supervisory and management roles.

The environmental benefits of sustainable forestry are increasingly recognized by consumers and regulators. Sustainably produced wood products sequester carbon, reducing the carbon footprint of construction and manufacturing. The use of wood in construction is growing as architects and builders seek to reduce the embodied carbon of buildings. The region's hardwood forests can supply the growing market for mass timber products such as cross-laminated timber (CLT) and glue-laminated timber (glulam), which are used in mid-rise and high-rise buildings. The development of a mass timber manufacturing facility in the region would create jobs and expand markets for the region's timber resources.

The future of the timber and wood products industry in the region is likely to involve greater integration of sustainable forestry practices, increased use of technology in harvesting and processing, and a focus on high-value products that can be sold to discerning customers. The region should position itself as a center for sustainable wood products in the Eastern United States, building on the presence of Appalachian Sustainable Development and other organizations that have pioneered the sustainable forestry model. The development of a wood products cluster can create a self-reinforcing cycle of investment and growth that benefits the entire region.

For investors, the timber and wood products sectors offer several attractive characteristics. The resource is renewable, creating a sustainable source of raw materials. The region's hardwood forests are among the most productive in the world, giving it a competitive cost position. And the growing demand for sustainable wood products creates a market opportunity that can be captured by the region's manufacturers. The key is to identify specific opportunities where the region's resources align with market demand, and to execute those opportunities with disciplined management and realistic financial projections.

# Conclusion: A Vision for the Future

The Abingdon-Bristol region stands at a crossroads. The decline of traditional industries—coal, textiles, furniture—has created economic hardship and population loss. But the region also possesses significant assets: a strategic location on Interstate 81, a skilled workforce with a strong work ethic, natural resources in agriculture and forestry, and a quality of life that appeals to workers and entrepreneurs. The challenge is to leverage these assets to build a diversified economy that creates opportunities for the next generation.

The advanced manufacturing sector, anchored by Electro-Mechanical's switchgear operations, provides a foundation for growth. The transition to SF6-free technology and the digitalization of switchgear create opportunities for innovation and differentiation. The region should support the growth of existing manufacturers and attract new ones by investing in industrial sites, workforce training, and infrastructure. The development of a manufacturing cluster can create a self-reinforcing cycle of investment and growth.

The agriculture and food processing sector offers opportunities to add value to the region's agricultural production. The beef price boom provides a window of opportunity to invest in processing infrastructure that captures more of the value chain. The development of meat processing, packaged food manufacturing, and craft beverage production can create jobs and expand markets for local agricultural products. The region should support farmers and processors by providing technical assistance, access to capital, and connections to markets.

The timber and wood products sector represents a renewable resource that can support sustainable economic development. The sustainable forestry model pioneered by Appalachian Sustainable Development demonstrates that processing timber locally creates more jobs and economic value than exporting raw logs. The region should support the expansion of sustainable forestry and wood products manufacturing by providing technical assistance, access to capital, and connections to markets.

The partnerships and investments described in this book provide a roadmap for economic expansion. The region's economic development organizations, educational institutions, and private companies must work together to implement this vision. The challenges are significant, but so are the opportunities. With strategic investments, effective partnerships, and a commitment to innovation, the Abingdon-Bristol region can build a prosperous and resilient economy that honors its past while embracing its future.
